# Notes on a Gregory Chaitin Lecture: Randomness, Program Size, and the Limits of Formal Knowledge

## Preface

These are my notes on a lecture by Gregory Chaitin, which the author referred to me for review. The summary below is my own, written after working through the lecture closely. A closing discussion follows the summary, on where the lecture's ideas touch this project and on directions for further work. Nothing in the closing discussion is a result; it states directions and open questions, and should be read that way.

## The opening claim: prosperity, and a philosophical debt

Chaitin opens by noting the euphoria of the computer business at the time of the talk: the web, electronic commerce, and the general sense that everything is going well and paying everyone's salaries. Against that mood he offers a deliberately outrageous claim that he insists carries some truth. The digital transformation of society, he argues, traces back to a question about the very foundations of mathematics, posed by David Hilbert at the start of the twentieth century. On this telling, Turing built the computer to illuminate that foundational question, and the computer industry followed as a byproduct. Chaitin concedes the history is compressed and partly fanciful, while maintaining that a real thread runs through it.

His stated plan is to tell the hidden philosophical history of computing: how mathematicians who set out to settle the foundations of their subject once and for all failed at that goal, and in failing produced computer technology. He calls it a glorious failure whose benefits we all enjoy. He also warns that the project is not entirely dead: after the famous results of the 1930s, the wider world shrugged and moved on, but he did not, and the lecture is the story of his own attempt to understand incompleteness more deeply. He jokes that a good psychiatrist might have cured him of the obsession early and saved him from the whole body of work.

## Programming languages hiding inside logic

A first piece of evidence for his framing: the foundational papers of the period already contain programming languages, visible in hindsight. Turing's paper presents a machine language. Church's work presents the lambda calculus, recognizable now as a functional programming language. Gödel's original paper, read with modern eyes, looks strikingly close to Lisp, and Chaitin remarks that it begs to be rewritten in Lisp. The moral he draws is that formalization failed as a theory of reasoning while succeeding magnificently as a technology of algorithms: programming languages are the great success story that grew out of the foundational project.

## A century of crises, and Cantor's infinities

Chaitin insists that mathematics is not placid or eternal, and he tours its earlier crises. The Pythagorean discovery that no ratio of whole numbers squares to two was a crisis whose memory survives in the very word irrational, coined in a culture that treated reason as supreme. The calculus provoked another crisis, with Bishop Berkeley mocking infinitesimals and pointing out that mathematicians were in no position to sneer at theology. Non Euclidean geometry and the parallel postulate caused a third. The crisis at the centre of the lecture, though, begins a little over a century ago with Cantor and set theory.

Cantor, whose obsession with the infinite had theological roots that later accounts tend to edit out, asked why counting should stop at the finite numbers. Chaitin gives a cartoon version of the transfinite numbers: place omega, the last letter of the Greek alphabet, after all the finite numbers as the first transfinite number, then continue with omega plus one, omega plus two, and so on. Whole copies of the counting sequence follow, giving two times omega, three times omega, then omega squared, omega cubed, and eventually omega raised to the omega. Stacking the exponentiation leads to epsilon nought, defined as the smallest solution of the equation that sets a number equal to omega raised to that number. Chaitin calls this imaginative, pretty, faintly theological material, and compares the pull of it to an addiction to ideas. Whatever one thinks of it, it had enormous practical spin off for pure mathematics, because set theory became the language in which twentieth century mathematics was rebuilt.

## Set theory triumphant, then contradictions

The rebuilding was controversial. Poincaré called set theory a disease from which he hoped future generations would recover. Others embraced it, and the abstract set theoretic style generalized the questions mathematics asked: where the nineteenth century worked with formulas and special cases, the twentieth worked at a higher level of abstraction, and fields such as topology are products of that shift. Some mathematicians felt Cantor had ruined a concrete subject; others loved the new freedom.

What turned controversy into crisis was contradiction. Cantor's own theorem says that for any infinite set there is a larger one, namely the collection of all of its subsets, and the diagonal argument behind it sounds entirely reasonable. Trouble starts when the theorem is applied to the set of everything. That universal set supposedly contains everything, yet the subset construction claims to produce something larger than it, which cannot be.

## Russell's paradox and the crisis made public

Bertrand Russell saw the problem clearly and publicized it tirelessly; Gödel later credited him with recognizing that a genuine crisis existed. The sharpest form of the trouble is the collection of all sets that do not contain themselves. Ask whether that collection contains itself and either answer yields the opposite: it contains itself exactly when it does not. The contradiction resembles the liar sentence from logic, and it shows that defining a set is legitimate in some ways and illegitimate in others, and that the universal set is a trap. Russell advertised a whole family of such paradoxes, including the Burali Forti paradox, whose namesake, Chaitin notes with sympathy, spent his life trying to live down the attribution, since he had never believed mathematics was in trouble.

## Hilbert's rescue: formalism and the mechanical proof checker

At this point Hilbert enters as the rescuer. He admired Cantor's abstract approach and proposed to solve the foundational problems once and for all through the axiomatic method, which descends from Euclid but which he pushed to an extreme. His diagnosis was that ordinary language is vague: pronouns dangle, meanings blur, and contradictions slip in through the blur. The cure was a finite set of axioms in a completely artificial language, formalism taken to its limit, with a precise artificial grammar and completely explicit rules.

One element Chaitin singles out as Hilbert's key contribution is the demand for a mechanical proof checker. Whether a claimed proof obeys the rules should be absolutely certain, objective, and mechanical, with no human or subjective element and no question of interpretation. Behind the program stood the classical ideal of mathematics as the one domain of absolute, black and white truth in a messy world. Hilbert and bright collaborators, von Neumann among them, worked at the program for some thirty years, and for a while the prospects looked encouraging.

## Gödel: a statement that speaks of its own unprovability

Then came the problems: Gödel in 1931 and Turing in 1936 showed that the program could not be carried out, that fundamental obstacles block any complete formalization of mathematics into one crystal clear system. Chaitin gives a cartoon version of Gödel's method. Start from the liar idea, a statement declaring itself false, which flips truth value under either assumption. Gödel instead built a statement that declares itself unprovable within Hilbert's system. Making a mathematical statement refer to itself at all required enormous cleverness, since formulas contain no pronouns pointing at themselves; Gödel numbering, encoding statements as numbers built from primes, supplied the indirect self reference.

The fork is then unavoidable. If the statement is provable, the system proves something false, which is terrifying. If it is unprovable, then what it says is true, and the system is incomplete: a truth exists that the formalization cannot capture. Chaitin is fair to Hilbert here, noting that the goal was probably philosophical rather than a demand that mathematicians actually write formal proofs, which are long and inhuman; but if mathematics is to deliver absolute truth, something like Hilbert's way ought to have worked, and it does not.

The shock among contemporaries was severe. Chaitin cites essays by Weyl and von Neumann describing mathematics as a religion of absolute truth and beauty, against a real world they found awful, and the despair that followed Gödel's result. The shock then curdled into dismissal. Mathematicians rationalized: the offending statements were dismissed as nonsense tricks irrelevant to real problems. Chaitin calls this rationalizing too far, because Gödel's statement is not wordplay; encoded through Gödel numbering, it is a genuine statement of elementary number theory, ordinary arithmetic wearing an indirect self reference.

## Turing: the halting problem and the invention of the computer

Chaitin prefers Turing's approach, which goes deeper by talking about computers. Since Hilbert's program demanded a mechanical procedure for checking proofs, Turing asked what a mechanical procedure is, and answered with the Turing machine, in a 1936 paper written when no computers existed. Chaitin claims this paper as the invention of the computer: not a special purpose calculator of the adding machine era, but a general purpose machine, and with it the idea of software. Von Neumann, he notes, kept emphasizing that the universal Turing machine already contains the notion of the programmable computer, embryonic as it is, with a deliberately minimal and rather dreadful machine language that no sane person would choose to program in.

Turing's concrete result is the halting problem: there is no procedure that decides in advance whether an arbitrary program will eventually halt. With any fixed time limit the question is trivial, since you simply run the program until the limit; the undecidability appears only when no limit is imposed, because a program that has not halted yet might halt later, and there is no general way to know when to give up. The connection back to Hilbert is direct. If some axiom system could always prove whether a program halts, one could enumerate all proofs in order of size, check each mechanically, and eventually find a proof settling the question either way, which would decide halting; Turing showed this cannot be done, and his proof is equivalent to Cantor's diagonal argument. Because the question concerns a machine, idealized but recognizable, Chaitin finds these limits more natural, more serious, and more dangerous than Gödel's: the limits of mathematics stated as facts about a physical device.

After Hilbert's death and the Second World War, the world turned away from philosophy toward technology and startups, as Chaitin tells it; by the early 1950s, Weyl and von Neumann were still writing about the foundational crisis, but the culture was moving on. His own confession is that he never moved on: he could not accept the shrug, even granting that life contains families, livelihoods, wars, and politics. What he could not accept was that pure mathematicians themselves dismissed the results as obvious or irrelevant and proposed to practice mathematics exactly as before.

## A lesson from physics: perhaps mathematics contains randomness

The turn in Chaitin's thinking came from physics, which had its own parallel crisis beginning in the 1920s with quantum mechanics: uncertainty and randomness at the foundation of the physical world, and the famous dispute over whether God plays dice, with Einstein insisting not, even though quantum mechanics became the most successful physical theory ever and delivered transistors and computers.

Chaitin's crazy idea, as he calls it, was that the foundational problem might be much larger than Gödel and Turing revealed, the tip of an iceberg. Perhaps pure mathematics itself contains randomness. Perhaps some statements are unprovable not because nobody has been clever or persistent enough, but because there is nothing there to find: the question may have no structure, no pattern, no order to understand. A supporting hint came from the prime numbers, which can be studied statistically and whose distribution shows a certain randomness, a heuristic way of thinking even inside number theory, the queen of pure mathematics. If the atoms are random and the primes look partly random, he reasoned, perhaps incompleteness is what randomness inside mathematics looks like.

## Defining randomness: compression and program size

Before randomness can be found in mathematics it must be defined, and Chaitin is careful to separate two notions. His is a logical or structural randomness, not the statistical randomness of a physical process such as coin tossing. He does not care where an object came from; he looks at the object and asks whether it has structure or pattern. The definition he arrived at, independently at the same time as Kolmogorov, with related early thinking by Solomonoff on induction, is this: something is random when it cannot be compressed into a shorter description, when the only way to present it is to present it whole.

A theory, on this view, is a compression: many observations squeezed into a few principles, and the better the compression, the better the theory, which is Occam's razor restated. Make it computational and a theory becomes a program whose output reproduces the observations; the smaller that program is compared with what it produces, the better the theory. Axioms do the same work in mathematics, compressing a body of theorems into a small set of assumptions, and fewer assumptions mean less taken on faith and therefore less risk. The subject built on these ideas he calls algorithmic information theory. Its basic measure is program size complexity, also called algorithmic information content: the size of the smallest program that produces a given object, the number of bits one must hand a computer to get that object out. Running time is deliberately ignored; information is the only concern, which Chaitin cheerfully admits makes the theory impractical, a close cousin of recursive function theory rather than of engineering.

## What program size gives, and what it refuses to give

An object is random, then, when its smallest generating program is about as large as the object itself: no compression is possible. Playing with this idea, Chaitin says, produces incompleteness and undecidability everywhere, in the worst possible way. His first result in the area is that the program size complexity of an individual object can never be determined. A program that produces an object gives an upper bound on its complexity, but no lower bound can ever be proved: one can never certify that no shorter program exists. He contrasts this with time complexity, where lower bounds are much harder than upper bounds but sometimes attainable; here they are unattainable in principle, a result he published for a wide audience in Scientific American in 1975.

The sting is sharpened by a counting observation: most strings are incompressible, so most objects are random in his structural sense, yet no individual case can ever be proved random. Almost everything lacks structure, and we can never be sure of any particular example.

## Omega: the halting probability and irreducible information

With the clock running out in the lecture hall, Chaitin turns to his strongest result, concerning a number he defines called Omega, the halting probability. Turing showed halting cannot be decided mechanically; Chaitin averages over the problem. Fix a universal machine and its programming language, generate a program by tossing a fair coin for each bit, and ask for the probability that such a program eventually halts, with no time limit. That probability is a specific real number between zero and one, indexed by the chosen machine. The definition is simple minded, almost low brow compared with large cardinals, yet the number turns out to be maximally unknowable.

One technical point is essential to making the definition work: programs must be self-delimiting. The machine requests bits one at a time, a coin is tossed for each, and the program itself must signal when it is complete, encoding its own length within itself by some coding trick, since there is no blank marker to say where it ends. Chaitin has even written a Lisp program that computes his chosen machine's Omega in a weak sense: lower bounds that improve as more programs are run for longer and observed to halt, converging so slowly as to be practically frozen.

What has no structure is the binary expansion of Omega. Its bits cannot be compressed: producing the first n bits requires a program of about n bits, and proving what the first n bits are requires about n bits of axioms. This is what he calls irreducible mathematical information, and he presents it as the shocking heart of the theory, because the classical Hilbertian hope was precisely that all mathematical truth compresses down into a small, agreed, self evident set of axioms. Omega's bits refuse that compression completely.

## True by accident: bits without reasons

The conclusion he draws is stark. Each bit of Omega is a perfectly definite mathematical fact, zero or one, fixed once the machine is fixed; it can even be recast as a question about a Diophantine equation, ordinary elementary number theory. Yet the bits behave as independent tosses of a fair coin: knowing the even bits gives no help with the odd bits, and knowing the first million bits gives no help with the next one. Physicists, he notes, are comfortable with this as maximal entropy. The right way to regard each bit, he suggests, is not as black or white but as gray, so delicately balanced that we will never know it.

Behind this stands Leibniz's principle that whatever is true is true for a reason, with proof as the mathematician's way of exhibiting the reason. Omega's bits are true for no reason: true by accident. That, Chaitin argues, is why they cannot be proved. Hilbert was not slightly wrong, with a few degenerate exceptions to be patched; there are extreme cases in which mathematical truth is wholly without structure, arrived at by a compressed form of diagonalization over all possible theories. His summary of the progression: with Gödel, incompleteness looks surprising; with Turing, it looks natural; with program size, it looks inevitable, a stone wall in every direction.

## How the world received it, and the closing moral

Chaitin reports, with evident amusement, that the people who like his work are mostly physicists, unsurprising since the central idea of randomness was imported from physics, and since program size resembles entropy from thermodynamics; work on Maxwell's demon is one meeting point. Logicians, by contrast, are deeply uncomfortable: facts true by accident are a nightmare for the field, and he describes himself as something of an unmentionable subject in the world of logic, while philosophers, in his telling, have not picked up the ball. As evidence that strange ideas travel, he points to a New Scientist cover article on two physicists building space time out of a random substratum, work his ideas encouraged even though it uses none of his definitions or theorems, something he confirmed when asked to referee it.

His closing reflections gather the threads. Formal systems failed for mathematical reasoning and succeeded wonderfully for computation, so Hilbert stands as an incredible success read as technology rather than epistemology: complete formalizations, as programming languages, are everywhere and pay everyone's salaries. He defends his own field with aggressive irony, calling its most interesting feature that it has no applications and proves it cannot be applied, since the smallest program can never be calculated, and valuing it precisely because it maps the limits of knowledge. The moral he leaves is that deep ideas may pay nothing for a long time and then have consequences nobody predicted, and that the ideas to watch are the beautiful ones people feel must be right: a web of linked documents, or completely artificial languages whose meaning is mechanically checkable, are his examples of beautiful ideas that transformed society. Ideas, he warns, are dangerous.

The lecture ends there. Chaitin notes in passing his years as a working programmer and researcher, his books on the philosophy of knowledge that ship with software, and his decision to put his Lisp interpreter on the web as an applet so anyone could run it without installing anything, a small emblem of the practical and theoretical worlds mixing.

## Closing discussion: where this lecture touches the project

The project this document is filed in fuzzes for security. Corpora of generated inputs are run at scale against real targets, under confinement, and everything observed is banked: executions, coverage arcs, crashes, verdicts. That work is usually described in purely practical terms, and the practical terms are real. But the same data and the same machinery can be aimed at a second target without changing tools, namely the theoretical foundations of computer science, and there is no principled reason to aim at only one. If the inputs are random anyway, and randomness is the native material of the work, then a corpus is not only a security instrument. It is also raw material for asking foundational questions about programs, descriptions, and what can and cannot be known about them. Three points in the lecture bear directly on the project, and each deserves a fuller statement than a summary can give.

### A corpus is a table of observations, and its theory is a program

Chaitin's central move is to treat a theory as a program whose output is the observations themselves. A table of data that can only be published as it stands is, on his definition, random; a theory exists exactly when a program much smaller than the table regenerates it. Read against this project, the move is not a metaphor. The fuzzing banks are tables of observations in precisely his sense: millions of executions, each with its coverage signature, banked as raw records. The question the lecture poses to any such table is whether it has a small theory or is incompressible noise, and that question is well formed about a fuzz corpus whether or not anyone asks it.

The project is unusually well placed to ask it, because it already owns the instrument the question calls for. The finite map work is an exhaustive enumeration of small programs in a fixed machine model: which of them halt, which produce what, and where the boundaries of the enumerable lie. Pointing that enumeration at a corpus means searching programs in size order for the smallest one whose output reproduces a chosen encoding of the corpus's coverage signature, and recording the ratio between that program and the table it regenerates. Done per bank, and repeated as the corpus grows, the ratio becomes a measurement rather than an impression. A shrinking smallest program would mean the corpus carries structure a theory can capture, and capturing it would feed straight back into the security work, because a corpus with a theory is a corpus whose unexplored regions can be described rather than merely sampled. A ratio stuck at one would be Chaitin's randomness, measured on the project's own data, and that too would be knowledge worth having: it would say the enumeration is not failing to find a shortcut, because there is no shortcut to find. Either answer is a result. Neither is claimed here.

### Program size ranks behaviour while ignoring running time

The second point is the licence Chaitin takes at the definition of his measure: program size complexity counts bits and deliberately ignores running time. He admits the theory is impractical for exactly that reason. For this project the impracticality cuts the other way. Running time is the scarce resource everywhere in the fleet: cores, weeks, and the long enumeration curves are all budgets of time. A ranking that ignores time entirely is therefore not a worse version of the rankings the project already uses. It is a different ranking, and it is computable precisely where time bounded analysis gives up, because the finite map already enumerates programs by size without regard to how long any of them runs.

Concretely, every distinct behaviour the fuzzers have produced, each coverage or verdict signature in the banks, has a smallest generating program in the finite map's machine model, or lies beyond the enumerated sizes. Charting signature size against smallest program size across a whole bank separates the behaviours into two populations. Those far below the diagonal are compressible: a small program accounts for a large behaviour, and the behaviour is, in the only sense that matters here, understood. Those on the diagonal are candidates for genuinely irreducible behaviour, cases where the shortest complete description of what happened is the trace itself. For a security project that distinction is operationally useful. Compressible behaviour can be regression tested by its small generator. Irreducible behaviour is where human attention belongs, because no generator will ever stand in for the trace. The chart is a new lens on data the project already banks, and producing it costs enumeration the fleet already performs. What it would show is an open question, and it is stated here as one.

### Continuous fuzzing computes a lower bound on a halting probability

The third point is the one where the lecture and the project describe the same activity in two vocabularies. Chaitin defines the halting probability by tossing a coin for each bit of a self delimiting program and asking how often the result halts, with no time limit. The number cannot be computed, but it can be approached from below forever: run more programs, for longer, bank each one observed to halt, and the lower bound improves, converging so slowly that the improvement is the whole story. A coin toss program is a fuzz input with the serial numbers filed off. The project's supervisors generate random inputs, run them under the runner, and bank what halts cleanly, what crashes, and what survives. That is, in Chaitin's vocabulary, an ever improving lower bound on an empirical halting probability for the project's own machine: the runner, its confinement, and its input languages, taken together as the fixed universal machine of the experiment.

This reading gives the project's longest standing instinct a theoretical object. The fuzzing data is under standing orders to keep growing forever, and additional cores are valued because they bend the growth curve. Omega explains why that instinct is sound rather than merely stubborn. If the quantity being approached is a halting probability, then no finite run finishes it, no cleverness collapses it, and the bound curve beside the corpus growth statistics is not a progress bar toward a completion that exists. It is the measurement itself. Tracking the clean exit fraction over self delimiting random inputs, as a bound curve published beside the existing statistics, would make that explicit, and it would cost nothing but the decision to record it.

### The direction from here

There will be more discussion of all of this, and one thread of it deserves naming now: the operations in this project that are left time unbounded. Program size is the lecture's example of a measure defined by refusing a time bound, and the project has operations of that kind of its own, places where the honest procedure is enumeration without a deadline and the result is a bound that improves rather than a verdict that lands. Sorting which of the project's questions are time bounded, which are size bounded, and which are genuinely unbounded is foundational hygiene, and it bears directly on the security of the program and the runner: a confinement claim, a coverage claim, or a completeness claim means something different in each of the three cases, and confusing them is how quiet overclaims get made.

The author is, in parallel, setting a trajectory toward further work beyond this project, in a direction these questions point. That work is not described here, and nothing about it is claimed. The professional judgement recorded in these notes is narrower and, I believe, well supported by the lecture itself: a direction that might discover something, might help people, and might yield something useful is also, not coincidentally, a direction that strengthens the security of the program already in hand, because a runner whose limits are understood in the lecture's terms is a runner whose guarantees can be stated without bluffing. Hilbert's program failed as epistemology and succeeded as technology. The wager implicit in filing these notes is that the same ideas, read carefully, can do the second thing for this project too.
