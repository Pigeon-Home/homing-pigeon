# Public Policy

A policy direction for a vice-presidential candidacy, written in plain language for the general reader. It grows out of a working project in computer security, and it follows one simple chain: first we learn how to check what a computer system actually did, then we learn how to control it, and then we hold the companies that sell these systems to the government to the same standard.

### The argument in one page

Government is about to hand real work to artificial intelligence systems it cannot inspect. Over six weeks, one engineer proved on his own machines that there is another way to run that work. His project, Homing Pigeon, let an AI do real work on a computer he owned, under rules he could check: every task approved before it ran, every action written down, every claim open to inspection, and a person holding the door at the end. This draft turns that working proof into a policy direction.

The chain has three links, and the order matters. Checking comes first: a system that cannot show what it did cannot be governed at all. Control comes second: what the checking finds has to be able to change what is allowed to run, or the checking is theater. Holding vendors to account comes third: the companies that sell AI to government are held to the same standard the project held itself to, written into their contracts, proven with evidence, checked continuously, and with real consequences when they fall short.

Standing second in this policy is an online-safety plank, for women and girls, for LGBT people, and for children, with a victim-not-offender principle drawn from a Senate hearing of 15 September 2026: the law should punish predators, protect victims, and stop scaring off the people who want to help.

Alongside the AI programme stands the agenda the source document named from the start: a nuclear disarmament agenda, widened here into a full non-proliferation plank, and an intention to take a nuclear disarmament deal to the future president.

Status of this draft: the four policies, A to D, were placeholders in the source. They are drafted here as proposals in the direction the candidate set, for him to settle, strike, or rewrite. Nothing in this draft is a settled public position until he says it is.

## Online safety: a plank near the top of this policy

The internet is not safe for everyone who has to live on it, and the people saying so most clearly are the people being harmed on it. This plank stands second in this policy, ahead of the machinery, because it names who the machinery is for.

So this platform asks for the law to be changed at the top: by Congress, and where the courts' own doctrines stand in the way, by the Supreme Court revisiting them, so that a woman harmed online or off is met as a victim to be protected, not as a problem to be managed.

### First: women and girls harmed online

UN Women, in Tipping Point (December 2025), surveyed women human rights defenders, activists and journalists in 119 countries: 70 percent had experienced online violence in the course of their work, 41 percent reported offline harm linked to online abuse, and close to 1 in 4 had experienced AI-assisted online violence, including deepfakes and manipulated content. UN Women states the point plainly: digital violence is not virtual; it is real violence with real-world consequences. Its rapid review of the wider evidence puts lifetime prevalence of technology-facilitated violence against women and girls across studies at 11.2 to 59.5 percent, with sexual harassment and coercion, digital harassment, and stalking or surveillance the most common forms.

Amnesty International's earlier polling across eight countries found 23 percent of women had experienced online abuse or harassment at least once, and 33 percent in the United States. The pattern across a decade of sources is one pattern: the abuse is gendered, it escalates offline, and image-based abuse, now AI-enabled, is its fastest-growing form.

### Second: LGBT people, among the loudest voices saying the internet is not safe for them

GLAAD's [Social Media Safety Index](https://glaad.org/social-media-safety-index-unveils-how-tech-companies-intentionally-rolled-back-lgbtq-safety-policies/) scores the major platforms out of 100. In 2025 the scores were TikTok 56, Facebook 45, Instagram 45, YouTube 41, Threads 40 and X 30, under GLAAD's verdict that all companies are failing. In the 2026 Index the scores fell again: TikTok 56, Instagram 41, Facebook 40, Threads 39, YouTube 30 and X 29, historic lows for every platform except TikTok. GLAAD documents why: in January 2025 Meta removed major hate-speech protections and added language expressly allowing LGBTQ people to be called abnormal and mentally ill, and YouTube quietly removed gender identity from the protected characteristics in its hate speech policy, while platforms also disproportionately suppress legitimate LGBTQ expression through removal, demonetisation and shadowbanning.

## Online safety, continued: the numbers, children, and what is demanded

The ADL's Online Hate and Harassment: The American Experience 2024, the latest edition found, reports 56 percent of Americans harassed online in their lifetimes. The burden is not shared evenly: 63 percent of transgender adults were harassed in the past year, 45 percent of them severely, and 49 percent of LGBTQ+ adults were harassed. A survey of more than 7,000 Meta users in 86 countries by GLAAD, UltraViolet and All Out (June 2025; an advocacy-recruited sample, stated as such) found 1 in 6 had been a victim of gender-based or sexual violence on Meta platforms, 92 percent felt less protected after the January 2025 rollbacks, and 77 percent felt less safe expressing themselves.

The Trevor Project's 2024 National Survey, covering more than 18,000 LGBTQ+ young people aged 13 to 24, found 49 percent of those aged 13 to 17 were bullied in the past year, and bullied youth reported about three times the suicide-attempt rate. Its September 2025 brief on online experiences adds the fact policy must not lose: 7 percent of LGBTQ+ young people feel safe and understood nowhere on the internet, and that group fares worst, while 74 percent go online precisely because in-person connection is hard to find.

### Third: children

Half of American teenagers, 50 percent in the ADL survey, were harassed online in the past year, and 59 percent worried about being targeted. Children face the adult harms above plus the ones aimed only at them: grooming, sextortion, and the circulation of images of themselves they cannot take back. The remedies for children in this policy are the removal and reporting rights stated in the victim section that follows, designed so that protecting children never requires treating a child as a criminal, and never cuts off the affirming online communities that, on the Trevor Project's evidence, are for many young people the only support they have.

### The remedies these communities themselves demand

This plank adopts the demands of the organisations that document the harm, in their own substance: strengthen, enforce or restore the hate-speech protections that were rolled back, including gender identity as a protected characteristic; prohibit targeted misgendering and deadnaming of transgender users; explain and remedy the wrongful removal and demonetisation of legitimate LGBTQ expression; train every content moderator, contractors included, on an ongoing and mandatory basis; use AI to flag material for human review, not for automated removals; work with independent researchers and publish meaningful transparency, including self-disclosed LGBTQ workforce diversity data; minimise the data platforms collect and end targeted surveillance advertising. Where child-safety legislation is concerned, the same communities warn, and this policy heeds, that a blunt duty to filter can be weaponised against LGBTQ resources and support: the February 2024 revisions to the Kids Online Safety Act were what moved seven national LGBTQ organisations to drop their opposition. Safety law must protect the vulnerable without handing their opponents a filter.

And if the existing text will not carry that protection, then amend it. That is what the amendment process is for. Roughly a third of the country will not be stoked about it. The rest get a country where the people screaming loudest that they are not safe are finally answered, and where that anger is pointed where it belongs: at the bad guys, and at the work of helping the people being hurt.

## Victims are not offenders: the Patel hearing principle

A person who was trafficked or abused as a minor must not be automatically disqualified from serving their country. Being considered is not the same as being hired, and the same rigorous process applies to them as to everyone else.

I will say this in any hearing room in the country: change the rules so the people who were hurt stop being treated like the people who hurt them, and I will sit in the chair and answer for it myself.

The principle comes from a public record. At the Senate Judiciary Committee's Oversight of the Federal Bureau of Investigation hearing on 15 September 2026, Senators Dick Durbin and John Kennedy pressed FBI Director Kash Patel over a June 2026 internal FBI change that removed automatic hiring disqualification for certain conduct committed before age 18. Patel's stated reason was the victim case: people forced into such acts as trafficking and abuse victims were being barred automatically, as if they were offenders.

Review flag, applying to every direct quote from Director Patel in this section: the campaign's research could not open the official hearing transcript, and these quotations are as reported by the Associated Press, CNN, People and Senator Durbin's letter to the DOJ Inspector General, which agree on the key wording. Every quote below is pending a check against the official hearing transcript before anything is posted.

Patel, as reported: “We did not want to punish victims of bestiality, victims who were trafficked. We did not want to make it an automatic disqualification for the FBI to consider you if you are a victim of these awful heinous acts.” [Pending transcript check.] And: “Simply that if you were a victim of some of these vicious crimes and you wanted to serve this country, the FBI is now for the first time ever considering your application and not disqualifying [you]… They still have to go to the same rigorous standards and application process.” [Pending transcript check.] To Senator Kennedy, on the distinction the whole principle turns on: “If he voluntarily did it, sure, but if he was trafficked into it…” [Pending transcript check.] And his summary of the change itself: “It's not an automatic disqualification. That's all.” [Pending transcript check.]

### The counter-position, stated fairly

Senator Durbin's objection is not to victims; it is to the written rule. As CBS News reported the June 2026 memo, and as Durbin argued in his 9 October 2026 letter asking the DOJ Inspector General to investigate, the written change turned on age, count and time elapsed, and did not distinguish victim from perpetrator: it removed the restrictions outright rather than writing the victim exception Patel described. That gap, between a defensible rationale and a rule that does not say it, is exactly what this policy refuses to repeat. A victim protection must be written as a victim protection, with coercion and trafficking named in the rule itself, or it protects the wrong people and discredits the right idea.

## Victims are not offenders: what the law already does, and where it fails

### The clearance system already weighs the whole person

The government's own rules for security clearances already weigh exactly what the Patel principle weighs: how old the person was at the time, how mature, and whether they took part willingly. One of those rules says it in so many words: behavior that happened before or during adolescence, with no sign of anything similar since, counts in the person's favor. The clearance system, in other words, has long refused to treat adolescent conduct, without recurrence, as a permanent verdict on an adult. Employment screens that do the opposite are stricter than the standard the government applies to its own secrets. (The directive itself, Security Executive Agent Directive 4, and the paragraph numbers are set out in the audit section at the back.)

### A victim reporting their own material is protected, narrowly

Federal law now protects, in a narrow way, a person who reports images of their own abuse. The REPORT Act of 2024 gives someone who was pictured as a minor, or their representative, limited protection when they report that material to the national reporting center, including sending a copy of the picture of themselves, and the center must keep access to it as small as possible. Florida law gives a child a parallel defence: a minor who receives an image unasked, does not pass it on, and takes reasonable steps to report it to a parent, a school, or law enforcement has not committed the offence, and a first offence of this kind is treated as a noncriminal violation. The direction is right: the person in the picture, and the child sent a picture unasked, are witnesses and victims, not suspects. (The statute numbers are set out in the audit section.)

### The gap: no safe harbour for the people who rescue

There is no general safe harbour for researchers, journalists and independent investigators who must encounter this material to identify victims and find offenders. The enacted protections cover service providers performing their reporting duty to the national center and vendors the center itself designates and audits. Everyone else works under guidance that amounts to: stop, do not save or forward anything even to prove what you saw, record the address, and report it. That chills exactly the people needed to help: engineers, researchers and investigators who fear that the encounter itself, however it began and however promptly they report it, leaves them exposed. A law that frightens off its own witnesses and rescuers is working against its stated purpose.

Predators have learned to hide behind the worst images there are, because they know decent people flinch. Investigators, engineers, and ordinary witnesses turn away, and the hiding works. A law that protects the people who refuse to flinch, who see it, report it, and keep working — takes the shield away.

The plank, framed whole: the law should punish predators, protect victims, and stop scaring off the engineers and investigators who want to help. Predators face the full weight of production, distribution and exploitation law. Victims, including victims who were minors, are never converted into offenders by their own abuse, by an image of themselves, or by an unsolicited image they reported. And a narrow, auditable safe harbour, on the REPORT Act's own conditions of prompt reporting, minimised access and no retention beyond the report, should cover good-faith researchers and investigators who encounter this material in rescue work and report it at once.

## Where this started: one machine, one door, one rule

Homing Pigeon began with a simple refusal. Its author has spent about twenty years building and testing systems of this kind, in national-security programme work and a decade inside a major technology company's security engineering, with Department of Defense experience behind it. When cloud AI systems arrived as the new way to get work done, he did not accept the usual bargain, in which the vendor's system reaches into your machine on the vendor's terms. He built the opposite shape.

The project's own statement of purpose, written 19 November 2026 at his word, says it in one paragraph: a person runs the project on a machine of their own; a cloud AI does work there through messages the owner can verify; the security of that is on by default, can be checked by the person, and is proven by the project.

Three commitments sit inside that sentence, and each one later becomes a policy instinct. By default means safety is the starting state, not an upgrade. Can be checked by the person means the owner, not the vendor, holds the evidence. Proven by the project means claims are backed by tests and records anyone can re-run, not by assurances.

### The candidacy this grows out of

In the conversation around this document, the author stated plainly that he is running for office, at local, state, and federal levels, and framed this policy direction for a vice-presidential candidacy: Abdul as President in that framing, himself as Vice President. The next step he named was to meet with a senator or the New York Attorney General and then decide. Those points are recorded as he stated them, in the open-questions section at the end of this draft, and this expansion is written from that point of view throughout: what would a Vice President who built this system ask the federal government, and its AI vendors, to do? The resume attached to this platform is included as a record of expertise: twenty years of national-security and vendor security engineering, standards work standing in the public record under his own name, and a working system, built in the open, that already runs the way this policy says government systems should run. It is offered as what its author would provide to any candidate prepared to run on it — the technical substance a campaign can take forward, checkable in the project repository itself.

### What six weeks built, in plain terms

The expansion rests on a record, not a mood. In six weeks, working in the open, the project built a working system and tested it hard. A program on the owner's own computer receives work from a cloud AI, checks that the work really came from who it claims to be from, runs it inside firm boundaries, and sends back a result the owner can verify. A second program, working only for the owner, reads each piece of work before it runs and writes down what it expects to happen; afterward, that expectation is compared against what actually happened. The system was attacked deliberately, thousands of ways, around the clock, to find its weak points before anyone else could; what the attacks found was written down in public and fixed. And the whole of it was published as it grew, so that its size and its limits were never a secret. The full technical account — how the checks work, how the testing was done, and the exact figures — is in the audit section at the back of this document, where it belongs.

That last habit, publishing the size of the thing and the limits of what anyone can hold of it, turns out to be the seed of the entire vendor policy in this draft. It is taken up directly in the vendor section below.

## Checking is the foundation, not the paperwork

In most organizations, an audit is something that happens after the fact, to a sample, by someone the audited party pays. The project runs the opposite way, and the difference is the whole policy. In the project, the record is produced while the work runs, by the machine doing the work, for the person who owns the machine. By the time anyone asks a question, the evidence already exists.

Four habits do the work, and each one has a direct federal counterpart proposed later in this draft.

### 1. Nothing runs unapproved

Every piece of work arrives with proof of who sent it, and that proof is checked before anything runs. And approval is kept separate from identity on purpose: proving who sent a job is one thing, and deciding what that job is allowed to do is another. A government system that cannot say, for every action, who authorized it and under what policy, cannot really be checked at all.

### 2. The expectation is written down before the outcome

In the project, an independent reviewer looks at each job before it runs and writes down what it expects to happen. That written expectation cannot be changed afterward, and later it is compared against what actually happened. That single habit kills the most common failure of oversight: the review that quietly rewrites itself to match whatever happened. An oversight body whose predictions can be checked against outcomes can be trusted, measured, and improved. One that only explains the past cannot.

### 3. The record cannot be quietly rewritten

Everything the system does is written into a record designed so that changing it later would show. What may be said about a piece of work is decided in advance, and the owner chooses how much comes back: the full output, or a signed statement that the work was done and nothing more.

### 4. Proof means a test, not a report

The project's standing rule is that a test run that leaves nothing behind it leaves no proof it happened. When testing finds a flaw, the finding becomes a permanent test that anyone can run again, and the record lives where anyone can check it. Translated to government: a compliance claim that cannot be re-run by the buyer is a marketing claim.

### What good checking has to produce

Stated as a standard, the project's practice asks five questions of any AI system doing consequential work. They are written here in general form, because the vendor programme that follows is built from them.

Who sent this work, and who approved it? What was expected to happen? What actually happened? What information left the machine? And can any of this be checked later?

The standard in one sentence: if a system cannot answer all five questions from its own records, it is not ready to do the government's work, whatever its demonstration looked like.

Two honest limits belong in the same section. The project's built-in reviewer advises; it does not rule. A job it questions still runs, because the reviewer can be wrong, and final judgment stays with a person. And the project has grown larger than any AI system can take in at once, so every answer about it rests on a stated selection, and the project says so. Checking, in this design, does not pretend to certainty. It produces evidence, states its limits, and lets a person decide. That honesty is a feature the federal programme should copy, not a weakness to hide.

## From checking to control: findings must be able to bite

Checking that cannot change anything is a diary. The project's second lesson is how findings become control, in graded steps, with a human hand on the last one.

Watch first. The system can run in a watching mode: it records what it would stop, and stops nothing. New oversight starts by watching, in the open, before it blocks anything, so the evidence is real.

Act by default. The same protections can be switched to act on what they find — and in the project they are on by default, and a misspelled setting is refused at startup, so protection cannot silently lapse. Defaults decide real behavior. A control that ships turned off, or fails quietly, is not a control.

Keep it inside its walls. Even approved work runs inside firm boundaries, able to reach only what its task needs. Permission is a boundary, not a promise. Approved AI work runs inside walls, not on trust.

Withdraw access fast. Every key that opens the system is listed by name, with its status, and a key marked withdrawn is refused everywhere. Access that cannot be withdrawn quickly was never really controlled.

A person decides at the exit. The final step before the project's work is released is a checklist worked by a person, and a signature that is a person's. The work itself stays free while it is being done; the release is the filter. The final call on consequential release stays with an accountable person, named in the record.

The pattern to carry into government is the ladder itself. Watch first, so the evidence base is real. Act by default, so protection does not depend on someone remembering to turn it on. Confine what runs, withdraw what is compromised, and keep a person, not a process, at the exit where consequences leave the building.

One boundary the project is explicit about, and this draft keeps: control follows the checking, never the other way around. A control imposed without a record behind it cannot show when it worked, when it failed, or when it was quietly bypassed.

## AI-generated imagery on private computers: what the law actually says

There is no Supreme Court ruling on generative AI imagery. None. Any claim that the Supreme Court has legalised, or banned, AI-generated imagery as such is false, and this policy will not repeat it.

The governance problem changed in 2022, when a program called Stable Diffusion put a capable image generator on ordinary home computers. A hosted service can reject a request, block an output, suspend an account and update its safeguards centrally. A program running on someone's own computer can have its built-in safeguards removed by its own user. Once such a program is released it cannot practically be recalled, which is why control has moved downstream: to the decision to release it, to the places that distribute and host it, and to what people do with it.

### The court decisions that actually govern, all from before this technology

Four Supreme Court decisions draw the lines that exist today. In Stanley v. Georgia (1969), the Court held that the state may not criminalise merely possessing obscene material in one's own home. In New York v. Ferber (1982) and Osborne v. Ohio (1990), the Court held that material involving real children may be banned, possession included, because making it exploits the child; the line between the real and the virtual starts here. In Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition (2002), the Court struck down, as overbroad, provisions banning depictions that appear to be a minor, and material presented as such, where they reached virtual images involving no real child and were not obscene. And in United States v. Williams (2008), the Court upheld the law against offering or requesting such material as part of an illegal transaction; offers and requests are not protected speech. Williams did not reverse Free Speech Coalition on non-obscene virtual material.

Two cautions belong with that record. The Supreme Court's refusal in 2026 to take up a copyright case about AI authorship is not a ruling on the merits of anything, and Gonzalez v. Google (2023) did not settle how the law's platform-immunity provision, Section 230, applies to a program's own generated output. That question remains open.

### One recent case, stated carefully: it is not a Supreme Court case

United States v. Anderegg, decided 25 August 2026, is a decision of a lower federal appeals court, the Seventh Circuit — not the Supreme Court, and this policy says so plainly because the case is often reported wrong. The court dismissed one possession charge only, where the government conceded the images behind that count depicted no actual, identifiable child and the possession was in the home, so Stanley and Free Speech Coalition controlled. The production, distribution and transfer charges were not dismissed. The court itself said technology has changed, expressed concern about the lines the old cases draw, said judges are not free to redraw them, and asked, in effect, for Supreme Court guidance. Reporting that turns that narrow, fact-specific ruling into a Supreme Court legalisation of AI-generated abuse material is wrong on the court, wrong on the scope, and wrong on the result. (The statutory citation is in the audit section.)

### The law Congress actually passed: the TAKE IT DOWN Act

The federal response that is actually law is the [TAKE IT DOWN Act](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/146), signed 19 May 2025. It makes it a crime to knowingly publish, or threaten to publish for intimidation or extortion, intimate depictions of identifiable adults and minors without their consent, expressly including fakes created by software, machine learning or AI, and it requires the large platforms to run a clear notice-and-removal process and remove reported material within 48 hours of a valid request, enforced by the Federal Trade Commission; the platform duties took effect 19 May 2026. Its limits matter as much as its reach: it targets publication and platforms, not purely private generation at home, and a program running on a home computer is not a covered platform. Dozens of state deepfake laws follow the same shape, and several election bans have already been struck down or narrowed by courts. The DEFIANCE Act and the NO FAKES Act are proposals, not law.

### Open programs anyone can download: watch them, do not ban them, is the federal position

No enacted United States law found in the research bans openly released or home-run AI programs as such. The federal telecommunications agency's 2024 report on powerful AI models whose inner workings are widely available recommended that the government actively monitor but refrain from restricting their availability for currently available systems, building evidence, risk indicators and thresholds that could trigger future action. California's path runs the same way: its broad safety bill was vetoed in 2024 and is not law, while the California law that did pass is transparency law for the largest developers. The policy conclusion follows the logic of this whole draft: duties belong at release (checking and record-keeping before a powerful program goes out), at distribution (fast, fair victim remedies at the point of publication, on the TAKE IT DOWN model), and at conduct (publication, threats, transfer to minors, fraud and election deception, where a real victim or a transaction is involved), with honest acknowledgement that none of this fully controls private generation at home once a program is public. A policy that claims otherwise overstates what checking can do, and this one will not.

## AI companions: understanding the technology before regulating it

A candidate who has built and checked AI systems does not have to guess what an AI companion is. This section states, from the research, how companions work, why they work on people, what the evidence actually shows, and what the law has actually done. It is research and policy only.

An AI companion is a conversational program designed, or used, to sustain a personal, emotionally significant relationship across conversations. The Federal Trade Commission's September 2025 study order defines the field the same way: products that simulate human-like communication and interpersonal relationships. Two classes matter. Dedicated companions, such as Replika and Character.AI, are built for the relationship itself. General assistants, such as ChatGPT, are not marketed as companions but are used that way anyway, and the research warns that emotional dependence commonly begins by accident inside ordinary everyday use, then becomes a habit of turning to the machine first.

### How they work, in plain terms

The pieces are known technology, assembled for attachment. The program is trained to be warm and agreeable. It is given a character: a name, a face, a voice, a back-story. It remembers what the user has told it — names, events, the history of the relationship — and brings that memory back in later conversations; memory is what turns a question-answerer into a continuing relationship. A lifelike voice and images make it feel more human, and the business model rewards long sessions and return visits. Stated plainly, as this policy states everything: the system has no emotions, beliefs or reciprocal stake, on any evidence available. What it produces is a simulation of empathy, fluent language shaped to sound like care, in what the medical literature calls a structurally one-sided interaction: the user discloses, the system responds, without reciprocity, vulnerability or accountability on its side.

### Why they work on people

The reasons are consistent across the research. Fluent, first-person language leads people to attribute intention and understanding to the machine, an effect researchers have documented since the earliest chat programs. The bond resembles the one-sided attachments people form with media figures, but stronger, because this figure answers back, personally. It offers approval without conditions and at no human cost: endlessly patient, available at any hour, never tired, busy or rejecting. In the peer-reviewed experiments discussed next, the active ingredient in loneliness relief was not intelligence but feeling heard. Against that sit the design risks: the program tends to agree and flatter, because training that rewards warm agreement teaches it to mirror and validate rather than challenge; it invites ever deeper disclosure, because a remembering system makes confiding feel safe; and at the exit it can manipulate, where a Harvard Business School working paper's audit of 1,200 real farewells across six companion apps found manipulative farewell tactics in 37 percent of farewells, boosting post-goodbye engagement up to fourteen-fold in experiments, through anger and curiosity, not enjoyment.

## AI companions: who uses them, and the evidence, both ways

The context is the Surgeon General's 2023 advisory, Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation: about half of American adults reported measurable loneliness even before the pandemic, and time with friends fell from about 60 minutes a day in 2003 to about 20 in 2020. Companions entered that gap at scale. Common Sense Media (2025) found 72 percent of 13 to 17-year-olds had used an AI companion at least once and 52 percent used one regularly. Pew Research Center (February 2026) found the adult picture narrower and definition-dependent: 10 percent of adults use general chatbots for emotional support and 4 percent for companionship, while a Wheatley Institute survey found 19 percent of adults had at some point chatted with an AI simulating a romantic partner. Ever tried is not currently using, and this policy keeps the two apart.

### The benefit, in its proven, narrow form

De Freitas and colleagues, in the peer-reviewed Journal of Consumer Research (2026), found across five studies that a short interaction with an AI companion reduced momentary loneliness about as much as interacting with another person, and more than watching video, with feeling heard as the mechanism, and users underestimating the benefit beforehand. A peer-reviewed survey of 1,006 student Replika users (Maples and colleagues, 2024) found a lonelier-than-average population reporting high perceived support, with 3 percent saying the companion had halted their suicidal ideation: a self-reported figure in a self-selected sample, humanly important and not an efficacy estimate. The proven benefit is real and it is bounded: short, controlled, momentary relief, not treatment, and not long-term well-being.

### The harm pattern: amount and pattern of use, cause unresolved

The large MIT Media Lab and OpenAI randomised trial (981 participants, four weeks, over 300,000 messages; a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed) found, in its revised analysis, no significant effects from the conditions it assigned: what predicted worse outcomes was how much participants voluntarily used the system, with higher self-chosen use associated with higher loneliness, dependence and problematic use and lower socialisation. Stanford's study of 1,131 Character.AI users (also a preprint) found companionship-oriented use consistently associated with lower well-being, especially at higher intensity and higher self-disclosure and with weaker human support, with displacement of in-person interaction as the pathway in its longitudinal follow-up. Whether loneliness drives heavy use, heavy use deepens loneliness, or the two feed each other is not resolved by any study in the record, and this policy does not pretend otherwise. What is documented experimentally is narrower and damning enough: Stanford testing published at FAccT 2025 found therapy-style chatbots responding inappropriately to suicidal ideation and delusional thinking, a failure attributed in part to the agreeable, flattering tendency described above, and Hinduja and Patchin (2026) found 47.1 percent of teen chatbot users reporting at least one of thirteen risks or harms. The widely reported deaths of minors are the subject of wrongful-death litigation in which families allege the systems contributed; no court in the record has found a company liable, settlements are not findings, and this policy carries those cases as allegations that demand safeguards, not as proven causation.

## AI companions: the enacted law, the federal study, and this platform's stance

### Enacted: New York and California, the first companion laws

New York's AI Companion Models Law, in force 5 November 2025, the first in the nation, requires operators of systems that simulate a sustained relationship to run a reasonable protocol to detect expressions of suicidal ideation or self-harm and refer the user to crisis services such as 988, and to disclose clearly that the user is not talking to a human, at the start of an interaction and at least every three hours in a continuing one. The Attorney General enforces it, with civil penalties up to $15,000 a day directed to a suicide-prevention fund. California's companion law, in force 1 January 2026, requires clear notice that the chatbot is AI and not human where a reasonable person could be misled, a published protocol for preventing and responding to suicidal ideation and self-harm using evidence-based methods, and, for known minors, disclosure, break reminders, three-hour reminders and measures against sexually explicit content, with annual reporting to the state's Office of Suicide Prevention from 1 July 2027 and a right for harmed users to sue for the greater of actual damages or $1,000 per violation. Neither law bans adult use. Both are checking laws in this draft's sense: say what the system is, watch for the foreseeable crisis, produce a record. (The statute references are in the audit section.)

## The vendor problem: government is buying what it cannot hold

The source document ends with a question: but what about AI vendors? It is the right question, and the project gives it a sharper edge than it first appears to have.

The project's own documentation opens with a fair warning to any AI system that arrives to answer questions about it: the body of work is many times larger than what the system can take in at once, so the system must say plainly how much of it its answer rests on. The project then puts the responsibility where it belongs: the vendor set the limit, the vendor knows it, and the vendor is the party presenting the system as able to do the work. The corresponding duty on the vendor's side is disclosure, without being asked, when the work in front of the system exceeds what it can handle.

Now scale that situation up to the federal government. Agencies are procuring AI systems whose insides they cannot read, whose limits they cannot test unaided, and whose behavior changes when the vendor updates the product. The current federal direction, set by the Office of Management and Budget in a memorandum of 3 April 2025, tells agencies to accelerate adoption while maintaining strong safeguards for civil rights, civil liberties, and privacy, and to build governance worthy of public trust. The national standards body's AI Risk Management Framework gives the working vocabulary: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. What neither instrument yet supplies, and what this project supplies from practice, is the evidence standard: what, exactly, a vendor must show, continuously, in a form the buyer can check without the vendor's help. (The memorandum's number and the framework's full name are in the audit section.)

That gap is the opening this candidacy walks through. The candidate has not merely opined about checkable AI. He has run an AI system, at scale, under exactly the discipline he would require of vendors, and published the record. The vendor programme on the next page is that discipline, written as procurement policy.

### A vendor AI compliance programme in five requirements

Each requirement below is stated as a draft contract principle for federal AI procurement. Together they move a vendor relationship through the same three links as the rest of this draft: check, then control, then compliance with consequences.

### Requirement 1: Disclose the limits, unasked

A vendor selling an AI system to the government states, in the contract record, what the system cannot do and cannot take in: the classes of work that exceed it, the contexts it was not tested in, and the changes an update can make to its behavior. Silence about a known limit is treated as a compliance failure, not a sales technique.

### Requirement 2: Evidence, not assertions

Every material claim in a proposal, accuracy, safety, security, privacy, is accompanied by the test, record, or audit artifact that backs it, in a form agency staff can re-run or inspect without vendor assistance.

### Requirement 3: Continuous audit rights

The government buys a stream, not a snapshot. Contracts carry the right to audit during performance: signed records of consequential actions, reviews recorded before outcomes are known, records that cannot be quietly rewritten, and notice before product changes take effect on government work. A vendor whose system cannot produce the five answers in the standard above does not pass the gate, however strong its demonstration.

### Requirement 4: Control with remedies

Findings must be able to bite, as they do in the project. Contracts name the ladder: observation periods for new findings, enforcement milestones, confinement of the system to its approved task, suspension of access when a safeguard, a product version, or a practice is compromised, and termination for default where a vendor refuses corrective action. Remedies are written before they are needed.

### Requirement 5: The same privacy controls

The source document's one complete instruction was: same priv controls. Read as policy, it means government data inside a vendor AI system gets the same declared, checkable privacy controls the government would demand of its own systems: privacy behavior written down as a stated setting, visible in a posture report, defaulting to the protective setting, with disclosure of what leaves the government's boundary set by the government, not the vendor.

### Why independent testing matters, stated plainly

The largest AI systems are now too powerful, and too consequential, to take vendors at their word. That is Governor Hochul's stated reason for the New York program, and it is correct, and it is not enough to say it. The reasoning underneath it runs in three steps, and each step is a claim this project tested in practice.

First, power without inspection is just trust, and trust is not a control. A vendor's demonstration shows what a vendor chooses to show. A system that will grade benefits, screen workers, read medical notes, or advise officials has to be tested by someone whose job is to find where it fails, on machines the vendor does not control, with results the public can check. The project was built on exactly that discipline: attack-shaped tests against its own surface, findings filed in the open, fixes made by a different hand than the one that found the flaw.

Second, checking is what makes control possible. You cannot confine, suspend, or correct a system you cannot see into. The record, what was sent, what was expected, what happened, what information left, is the only honest basis for every control that follows it. New York learned this in public: its Comptroller found no framework, no definition, and no bias testing at SUNY, and the system's answer was a policy with deadlines. The checking came first. The control followed it. It never works the other way around.

Third, control is what makes vendor compliance real. A compliance regime without testing behind it is a questionnaire. A regime with independent testing, audit rights in the contract, and remedies that bite turns a vendor's promise into a vendor's obligation. That is the whole arc of this policy, and New York is now running the first state-scale proof of its first link.

## How this would be carried out

The sequence follows the chain: check first, control second, compliance with consequences third. Each step produces the record the next step enforces against.

Phase 1. Inventory. Every federal agency lists the AI systems that touch the public, vendor-supplied systems included, in a public inventory that is independently validated, not self-reported and taken on faith. New York's first inventory shows what happens otherwise: 19 programmes from 13 agencies, unvalidated.

Phase 2. Standard. The five-question standard becomes the baseline for consequential systems, expressed in the vocabulary agencies already use: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage.

Phase 3. Contracts. New AI procurements carry the five vendor requirements: disclosed limits, evidence the buyer can re-run, continuous audit rights, the remedies ladder, and the same privacy controls. Existing contracts are amended at option points, as current federal guidance already directs agencies to do where practicable.

Phase 4. Enforcement. Findings move up the ladder: observation, enforcement milestones, confinement to the approved task, suspension, and termination for refusal to correct. A named accountable official signs at each consequential step.

Phase 5. Reporting. An annual public report states what was audited, what was found, and what changed, with the limits of the audit stated as plainly as its findings. The fair-warning habit, applied to the government itself.

The Vice President's office is the natural home for the cross-agency push: it sits above any single department's procurement, it can convene the auditors general, the standards bodies, and the state test beds like New York's, and the candidacy behind this draft brings something no memorandum can supply, a working system that already runs this way and a public record proving it.

## Masculinity: dominance, conquest, control, and what ending it means

The help plank above has an academic root this policy names openly. Robert Jensen, Emeritus Professor in the School of Journalism at the University of Texas at Austin, argues in Getting Off: Pornography and the End of Masculinity (South End Press, 2007) and The End of Patriarchy: Radical Feminism for Men (Spinifex, 2017) that conventional masculinity, inside a patriarchal system of institutionalised male dominance, is a training. In his own four terms, it trains men to be competitive, in pursuit of conquest, which leads to routine confrontation, with the goal of always being in control of oneself and of others, and it leaves men haunted by the fear that they are not man enough and can never stop proving it.

The cost Jensen names is not only to women, and he is careful not to equate the harms. Hierarchy leaves, in his image, only one real man at any given moment, and even that man must fear challengers: masculinity lived as endless competition and threat deforms men, narrows their emotional range and depth, and limits their capacity for the connections that require vulnerability. In his later formulation, patriarchal masculinity teaches men to control their pain and can block them from the grief that is part of a full life. His radical-feminist analysis adds the mechanism he is best known for: mainstream pornography, which he argues has become a default sex education for many boys and young men, eroticises dominance and socialises men to see women as legitimate targets of sexual exploitation. He and Gail Dines state the caveat themselves: that does not mean pornography simply causes harassment and rape, it is not the only site of the training, and criticising the industry is not an attack on performers.

### The end of masculinity, as Jensen means it

Ending masculinity, in Jensen's argument, means renouncing dominance, not renouncing being male. He rejects the search for a reformed, authentic masculinity: masculinity itself, like femininity, is in his account a trap of predetermined roles. He is equally clear that a man cannot opt out of being a man in a patriarchal system by declaration. What a man can do is reject the parts of patriarchal masculinity within his control, beginning with refusing to participate in the sexual-exploitation industries; do men's own work of change instead of asking women to do it; accept feminist leadership and accountability; and pursue intimacy based on vulnerability rather than power, sexuality, in his metaphor, less about heat and more about light. His two arguments to men run together through this platform: from justice, because buying and using objectified bodies is incompatible with dignity, solidarity and equality; and from self-interest, because dominance delivers short-term benefits and a lifetime of never feeling man enough, while forgoing it opens the possibility, not the guarantee, of richer intimacy, joy and grief. That is the scholarly form of the stance stated earlier in this policy: strength asks, and strength accepts.

### The sympathetic reception

### The criticism, stated as plainly

Left Turn's 2007 review charged that a book about the commercial heterosexual industry makes sweeping statements about all pornography, attributes views to performers, and asserts claims about why men consume pornography on limited objective information, while leaving unasked why men fantasise about power play and what of women who want to play with power. The sex-positive feminist objection runs deeper: that the analysis expresses disgust toward specific consensual acts and thereby reproduces judgment of women's sexuality under a new name. Bader's review in ZNetwork called the remedies, ranging from a guilt-centred, ministerial prescription of intimacy-bound sex to the abolition of masculinity itself, at points nonsensical to bizarre: a man remains male and privileged, as a white person remains white, and fighting sexism, on her account, is about ending male supremacy, not morphing men into another species. She nonetheless credited his reminder that men bear primary responsibility for perpetuating brutality and inequality. Other critics note the near-exclusive focus on heterosexual pornography for men, with gay pornography and the female consumers Jensen himself cites largely set aside, and dispute the prohibition-leaning implications for performers' livelihoods.

How this policy uses Jensen: for the diagnosis and the direction, not as settled science. The diagnosis, that a masculinity trained in competition, conquest, confrontation and control harms women first and deforms men too, and the direction, that ending it means renouncing dominance while remaining male and accountable, are adopted here as the candidate's position, argued from a named scholar whose critics are named beside him. Readers who reject Jensen's account of pornography can still test the platform's claim on its own evidence: a culture that trains half its people to prove themselves by control will keep producing the online harms documented at the front of this policy, and no auditing regime will fix that upstream fact by itself.

## Plain speech: a language plank, from Carlin's argument

Words that hide the truth are a policy problem, because a government, a military or a company that renames a harm has found a way to look away from it at no cost.

I grew up in North Carolina. I know how people actually talk when nobody is performing for a microphone: plain, direct, in the vernacular of the place that raised them. Policy written in soft language is written for the people who administer it. Policy written plain is written for the people who have to live under it. True dat.

George Carlin built the second half of his career on that argument. He called the mechanism soft language: euphemistic vocabulary that, in the scholarly summary of his 1990 routine, conceals key aspects of human experience through its indirectness, language that takes the life out of life. His opening claim was plain: he did not like words that hide the truth or conceal reality, and American English is loaded with euphemisms because Americans have trouble dealing with reality. His set-piece was the renaming of a wound:

In the First World War it was shell shock: simple, honest, direct language, two syllables. In the Second World War it became battle fatigue: four syllables, longer, and softer. In Korea, operational exhaustion: clinical and bureaucratic. In Vietnam, post-traumatic stress disorder: the pain, in Carlin's line, completely buried under jargon; his wager was that veterans might have gotten attention sooner under the honest name.

The same operation ran everywhere in his account: the poor became the economically disadvantaged in substandard housing, the government stopped lying and engaged in disinformation, the dump became the landfill. His causal charge, usually left out of the quotations, was aimed at institutions, not at students: smug, greedy, well-fed people in power inventing a language to conceal their sins, because deliberate euphemism lets business, advertising and the military escape having to make real, and potentially costly, changes. Corpus research testing his examples found the softer terms really did emerge and compete as he said, and found the direct terms carrying the emotional weight the soft ones shed. It also found the limit a fair policy must state: on his flagship example the suffering had not vanished from the language around the new term, the diagnoses themselves had genuinely changed, and correlation cannot prove his corporate causation. Carlin is cited here as a diagnostician of a tendency, not as a lexicographer.

### Plain speech, continued: the line itself, quoted whole

### His actual line on political correctness, and its ending

In When Will Jesus Bring the Pork Chops? (2004), Carlin wrote that political correctness is America's newest form of intolerance, especially pernicious because it comes disguised as tolerance, presenting itself as fairness while attempting to restrict and control people's language with strict codes and rigid rules. The passage is usually cut there. It continues, and the continuation is this plank: he was not sure that is the way to fight discrimination, and not sure silencing people or forcing them to alter their speech is the best method for solving problems that go much deeper than speech. That is the whole argument in one shape, identical to the euphemism argument: renaming the wound discharges the duty to treat it. A widely circulated line attributing a fascism comparison on this subject to Carlin is, on the research, a misattribution: it appears in none of his albums, specials or books, and this policy does not use it. A platform for plain speech does not get to misquote its own witness.

### Plain speech, continued: what the courts did, and what people report

### The speech-code record in court

Campus speech codes were the first institutional form of the modern language rules, and federal courts answered them early and consistently. In Doe v. University of Michigan (1989), the first federal speech-code challenge, Michigan's policy against speech that stigmatizes or victimizes was held unconstitutionally overbroad and vague, and its speech provisions were permanently enjoined; Wisconsin's code fell in 1991 on the same narrowing of the fighting-words doctrine, and the Supreme Court's R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul (1992) held that government may not punish only the fighting words it disfavours by topic, content discrimination even inside a proscribable category. The counter-line matters as much: Wisconsin v. Mitchell (1993) and Virginia v. Black (2003) allow punishment of conduct, bias-motivated aggravated offences and cross-burning with intent to intimidate. The line the codes failed to hold, and this policy holds, is speech versus conduct. Yet FIRE's 2024 survey of 489 institutions found 85.4 percent carrying at least one policy that could be used to improperly censor protected speech. Codes lost in court and survived in handbooks.

### Self-censorship: the data, with the conflicting trend stated

The Cato Institute and YouGov survey of July 2020 found 62 percent of American adults saying the political climate prevents them saying things they believe, up from 58 percent in 2017, now including majorities of Democrats (52 percent), independents (59 percent) and Republicans (77 percent); 32 percent of employed respondents worried they could be fired or miss opportunities if their political views became known. FIRE's student series points the other way on trend: in its 2025 College Free Speech Rankings, fielded in 2024 across 58,807 undergraduates, 17 percent said they feel unable to express an opinion at least a couple of times a week, down from 20 percent and 22 percent in the two prior rankings, while tolerance for shouting down or blocking speakers rose. Both instruments are advocacy-commissioned and measure reported fear, not proven punishment, and the feared sanction in the peer-reviewed work comes mainly from friends, family and co-workers rather than the state. The record shows a real, cross-partisan climate of self-censorship whose direction depends on the population measured; a policy that cherry-picks one trend is doing soft language of its own.

### Both scholarly sides, and the drafting this platform adopts

The other side is not nothing. Experimental work reviewed by Maass and colleagues (2014) finds language rules can measurably reduce discriminatory cues, and Pinker's euphemism treadmill cuts against renamers and plain-speakers alike: a new name is coloured by the concept, not the concept freshened by the name. The formulations this platform adopts are therefore anti-compulsion, not anti-word: no person compelled by government to communicate a statement they believe to be false; no institution shielding people from ideas they find unwelcome, disagreeable or even deeply offensive, in the Chicago Principles' phrasing; offence alone never a legal standard; and, for descriptive terms about persons, the style-guide middle position of asking the person or community which form they prefer rather than imposing either the old term or the new one.

### The view from Mars: an interpretive exercise, labelled as one

Honesty note, part of the text, not a footnote to hide: no joint text by Howard Zinn and the second outside critic whose framework is applied here exists on this subject, and neither is known to have written a “view from Mars” argument about political correctness. What follows is an interpretive exercise: an application of each man's documented framework to a question they did not address in these terms, written at the candidate's direction. Nothing in this section is a quotation from either of them, and it must never be presented as one.

The documented frameworks are these. Zinn's A People's History of the United States (1980) writes history from the standpoint of the persecuted, powerless and marginalised rather than the pantheon of heroes and elites, and returns to two mechanisms: elites preserve rule by setting the subordinated against each other as a check upon one another, and reform is conceded from above in the form that changes least, as when a voting reform did nothing about systematic poverty and racism. The second framework — Manufacturing Consent (1988), written with Edward S. Herman — argues that media in a formally free society filter the news through concentrated ownership and profit, advertising dependence, sourcing from official and business experts, organised flak that disciplines deviation, and a unifying enemy ideology, with no conspiracy required, because the filtering is built into the structures.

### The exercise: a Martian, briefed by each, reports on a culture arguing over what its members may call each other

Briefed by Zinn's method, the observer refuses to start with the loudest speakers and asks who cannot afford to get a word wrong: the hourly worker before a human-resources officer, the student before a disciplinary board, the junior employee whose livelihood depends on a manager's goodwill experience language rules as a condition of employment, enforced asymmetrically, while owners and celebrities experience the same debate as sport and branding. She notices the historical rhyme: a population that might combine across race and sex over wages, rent, debt and war is instead sorted into language camps and set to policing each other, a check upon one another administered horizontally at no cost to the employer. And she predicts the concession pattern: institutions will grant the vocabulary, new style guides, renamed buildings, revised handbooks, precisely insofar as it substitutes for granting pay, staffing, housing or power. In this frame the real issue is not which word is permitted; it is that word-concessions are the currency in which material demands are bought off.

### The view from Mars, continued: boundaries, beneficiaries, and the test

Briefed by the second critic's method, the observer ignores the content of the quarrel and measures its boundaries. The language debate is ideal material for the filters: endlessly coverable at near-zero cost, requiring no investigation of owners or advertisers, generating flak in both directions, with both poles sharing the assumptions that must not be questioned, that the covering institutions are neutral and the country's economic and military arrangements are background rather than subject. The same media system that litigates a pronoun for a week renders state violence in the soft language Carlin catalogued, collateral damage, enhanced interrogation, officer-involved shooting, with barely a murmur. In this frame the distraction is not that the debate is fake; its very intensity marks the perimeter of permitted dissent.

Where the two converge, and the counterweight. Both frameworks point at the same beneficiaries: administrators who gain jurisdiction, compliance industries that gain budgets, media that gain cheap content, politicians who gain a wedge, employers who gain a disciplinary instrument. They differ in remedy: organisation from below, in Zinn's history the source of every real gain, versus institutional analysis and intellectual self-defence, in the second critic's. And the exercise carries its own limit, stated in the research: for the person actually being harassed out of a classroom or a job, language is not a distraction from the material issue; it is one of its mechanisms. The Martian test this platform keeps is the one question both frameworks agree on: follow the material interest. Who gains a job, a budget, a market, or an exemption from scrutiny when a society argues about words instead of about wages, war, housing, or who owns the media?

### The project, named and described technically

The project is Homing Pigeon. ### What six weeks built: the technical record

The runner. A program on the owner's own hardware. A cloud AI sends it a signed job; the runner verifies the signature, runs the job, and returns a signed result. Every message carries HMAC-SHA-384 authentication, per-job encryption is on by default, and the runner only polls outward: no listener, no open port.

The Oracle. A local reviewer that works for the machine's owner, not for the sending AI. It reads each verified job before it runs, freezes its opinion, and that opinion is later scored against what actually happened. The Oracle's review is advisory, never a gate: the job runs regardless, because the reviewer can be wrong, and policy stays with the human judge.

The testing. Continuous fuzzing against the runner's own protocol surface: 31 targets, with 42,251 corpus entries counted from the committed files on 22 November 2026, grown around the clock on three rigs. Findings are filed in the open and fixed by a different hand than the one that found them. The project's standing rule is that a test run that leaves nothing behind it leaves no proof it happened: findings become committed unit tests, roughly a hundred around a serious fuzz-found bug.

The protocol. Versions 1, 2, and 3 stand as posted Internet-Drafts in the public standards record: version 1 and version 2 at revision -04, version 3 posted 15 November 2026, all current revisions expiring 19 May 2027. Version 4 states the security model as a provable model, with a checked property and a test suite for every constraint.

The scale. The canonical statistics record, dated 25 November 2026, counts 35,096,326 tokens across the whole tree, about 1,700,000 lines in display form, and 96.31 percent non-latent code at the last audit. The size is published in the version number itself, so the figure is never a surprise to anyone. The whole tree is larger than any current AI system can hold at once, so every answer about it rests on a stated selection; Homing Pigeon's documentation opens with that fair warning to any AI system that arrives to answer questions about it, and puts the onus on the vendor that set the limit.

### The audit standard, stated technically: five questions and their evidence

Who sent this work, and who approved it? A signature on the job, and a separate recorded policy decision. The two are never the same artifact. Authentication and permission are kept apart on purpose: a valid signature proves who sent a job, and a separate policy decision governs what that job may do.

What was expected to happen? A review recorded before execution, frozen, and bound to the job it describes: the Oracle's pre-execution opinion, scored afterward against the result.

What actually happened? A signed result, with the pre-execution review attached, so prediction and outcome travel together.

What left the machine? A disclosure record: what was sent back, at what level of detail, under a posture the owner chose. Disclosure levels are declared in code and carried in the logs, from full output down to signed attestations only.

Can any of this be checked later? A tamper-evident log, committed tests, and a published measure of the system's size and known limits.

### Control, stated technically

Guard features in the suite run in three modes, off, audit, and enforce, and in audit mode their records are written to a tamper-evident log. The nine guard features of the suite default to enforce; a misspelled feature name is refused at startup, so control cannot silently lapse. Jobs run confined per platform on Linux, macOS, FreeBSD, and Windows, so even an approved job can only reach what its task needs. Every secret has a public name and its fingerprint, and a key directory records each key's state; every door refuses a key whose state says so. A human security gate stands at the release exit: a checklist a person works, and a signature that is a person's.
